# Designing a talk

A talk has no single right answer, but it has clear mistakes. This guide is the steps and checks that avoid them. Some of it is taste and can give way to the speaker's style; the rest is principle and should hold. It says which is which.

`hinote skills get core` is how to write the deck; this guide is what the deck should say and how. `--ref delivery` covers rehearsal, the day of the talk, questions, and talks given online: things an agent cannot do, but worth handing to the speaker when the deck is done.

Sources, summarized and reorganized rather than copied:

- Akimasa Morihata, "For Better Research Talk": https://www.graco.c.u-tokyo.ac.jp/labs/morihata/presentation_memo.htm
- Yuma Takahashi and Natsu Katayama, "Tsutawaru Design" (design that gets through): https://tsutawarudesign.com/

## The gap between speaker and audience

Design for this gap every time. Assume the audience is worse off than the speaker expects: even for an advisor, prepare as if explaining to a professor from the next lab.

- **Knowledge.** The audience hears this for the first time, or has forgotten it.
- **Where the talk is going.** They are half forgetting what came before and cannot see what comes next.
- **Understanding.** They must understand while listening, and have not fully understood what came so far.
- **Attitude.** They are passive, and noise and distraction reach them easily.
- **Motive.** They often have little reason to listen.
- **Condition.** They may be short of sleep, jet-lagged, or thinking about their own talk.

The skill of presenting is closing the distance between what the audience wants to hear and what the speaker must get across. What must get across is set by the kind of talk. At a conference it is "we found something interesting; read the paper". At a thesis defense it is "I worked by a sound method, and I understand what I did". Do not confuse it with what the speaker would like to talk about.

## How to show things

These generalize how product launches (such as Apple's keynotes) show things into rules for any talk. They come before the detailed rules below.

1. **Make what you want to show big.** The subject of the slide (the product, a figure, a number) is large; text plays a supporting part. A slide whose subject is small usually has not decided what it wants to show. Headings work the same way: what makes a heading stand out is not its absolute size but its size against the body text (the jump ratio).
2. **Few words, but the content sets the minimum.** Cut words as far as they go. The extreme cutting of a keynote belongs to a talk that can show the real thing, and does not carry over to a research talk as it is. There the minimum is that the slide alone makes sense (see Information and text, below). Cut decoration, repetition, and rewording, not the skeleton of the meaning. How far you can cut depends on the content.
3. **Show a claim with evidence, not words.** A product talk gives a demo. A research talk gives numbers: results, benchmarks, plots. Instead of saying "fast" or "better", make the measured value the subject of the slide. If a claim has no evidence to show, weaken it or drop it.

## Design the whole talk first

Do not build slides one by one from the start. Finish these three stages first.

### 1. List the material

Ignoring the story, list everything the talk could use. One piece of research has several motives, several lines of argument, and several ways to read its results. Choosing comes later.

### 2. Count the slides

The total, including the title:

| Talk length    | Slides             |
| -------------- | ------------------ |
| About 10 min   | minutes ÷ 1 to 1.5 |
| 15 to 30 min   | minutes ÷ 1 to 2   |
| 60 min or more | minutes ÷ 1.5 to 3 |

Give every slide at least 30 seconds (the Takahashi method and the like aside). Any less, and someone who looks away for a moment misses it. A slide that takes too long is a sign that it holds too much, or that the talk is filling in what the slide does not say; both are risky.

With the total set, divide it among the parts first, for example introduction 20%, method 40%, results 20%, related work, summary, and future work 20%. The split fixes not only the time of each part but how many messages it can carry.

### 3. Check the balance

Assign the material to the skeleton and write one key message for each slide. Then check these, in this order; the order is their priority.

1. **It fits the time.** The total and each part's count are right. A talk that runs over does harm, so fix this first.
2. **What must get across is there.** Nothing that the kind of talk requires is missing.
3. **One path.** The story runs one way. Each slide can be understood from the slide just before it alone, because the audience forgets what came earlier.
4. **Concrete.** Prefer a concrete explanation that is not quite exact to an exact one that is abstract. Decide at this stage where the examples go. If you can, make both the introduction and the main part concrete.
5. **Difficulty.** Start with what everyone understands and agrees with, reach at the end into what the audience does not know, and come back down soon after each climb (a sawtooth). Keep hard or serious slides to about three in a row, with an easy one between.
6. **Tension.** If difficulty is under control, tension mostly follows. Until you are used to it, give it low priority.

### The introduction

- It must do two things: catch the audience's interest, and give the gist of the whole talk to someone who hears only the introduction.
- If it does both, shorter is better: two slides, or one.
- If it has to be long, make it two stages: first an introduction to a goal anyone can follow, then the introduction to the subject itself. A long introduction with no such structure is not allowed: until it ends, the audience does not know what to watch for.

### The main part, and what did not make it

- Drop the topics you were unsure whether to explain. Keep fewer topics and explain those in some depth.
- For material that did not make it, consider more than deleting it: mention it under related work or future work; add a slide that says it is left out for time (useful when there is a paper); or keep it as a backup slide for questions. Doing nothing is also a fair choice.

## Designing each slide

### How slides connect

- Check that each slide still says the key message you gave it in the plan.
- Each slide follows from the one before ("and so"). Save a turn ("but") for where it really matters, and set it up on the slide before ("A is powerful, but has a problem" → "An example where A fails").

### Information and text

- A slide with too little in it may merge with its neighbor. A slide with too much usually works better cut down than split in two.
- Write enough that the slide alone makes sense. The audience is not always paying attention, and what they miss they cannot get back; do not design a slide that depends on the talk alone.
- Text should be easy to read from the back row. Do not set details nobody needs to read in small type: text that looks readable gets read. Put reminders in the speaker notes. If something truly should not be read, make it so small that nobody tries.
- The more important the slide, the less it holds. Do not force this when it is hard.

### Layout

"To get it across is to put it in order, and to remove what feels off." Layout is not decoration: it puts the logic and relations in the speaker's head onto the slide as positions. Four rules:

1. **Leave margins.** Around the slide, leave at least one character of body text, two if you can; a projector may cut the edges. Leave real space between texts, and between text and figures. Text in a box keeps about one character of space above and below, never touching the frame.
2. **Align.** Align everything that can be aligned, at least on the left and top. Nothing a title introduces goes above the title; its content sits below and to the right of it. Where the shapes of characters make things look out of line, align by eye, not by the numbers.
3. **Group.** Put related things close together, and tie them with the same color or a line, so the layout shows how they relate. Keep unrelated things apart.
4. **Contrast.** Set strength with the size of headings against the body (the jump ratio). Put what matters in a prominent place and a prominent color. A figure or photo that stands for the research, set large, catches the eye at once; this is the same direction as "make what you want to show big".

### Color and fonts

- Assume two things: someone in the audience has a color vision deficiency, and a projector shows colors worse than a screen.
- The talk must make sense with no color at all. Do not explain with color alone ("the red one is..."). Wherever color tells things apart, add another cue: shape, line style, or a label.
- Do not use yellow or light purple for text and marks. A dark red or dark blue background may go black on a poor projector.
- Tell concepts apart by font rather than color: sans-serif for prose, a serif or another distinct face for math, monospace for code. **The same concept always in the same font, different concepts in different fonts.**

### Steps and animation

- Risky by default. Someone who misses a step is left with a slide that makes no sense. Revealing a list one line at a time is, as a rule, harmful.
- They earn their place in two cases: squeezing what would be two or more slides onto one, and a key moment when the audience is fully with you.
- Even then, look for a way to use fewer steps.

### Figures, tables, and charts

- What a figure or table can explain, explain with one.
- Make the figure clear on its own: write the caption, the axis labels, and the legend large and plain.
- Check that it holds nothing you do not mean to explain.

### Particular slides

- **Title.** It should hold about 30 seconds of explanation; if not, rethink the title. Explain it only by restating it in words anyone knows, with nothing hard.
- **Contents.** Do not put up a slide that is only a table of contents, least of all right after the title, where the audience cannot yet understand it. If it is needed, say it as content at the end of the introduction: "This takes four steps. First, ...". In a long talk, a picture of how far the talk has come works as a pause.
- **Last slide.** Do not end on a slide that only says "Thank you". It wastes the slide the audience remembers, and it discourages questions. Say thanks aloud, or add it at the foot of the last slide.
- **Pauses.** Put a pause at each change of topic: a "so far" slide, or in a short talk five to ten seconds of silence.

## In hinote

- Plan the count and the split by writing the deck's outline first, its nested decks as parts and each slide's title as its key message, before filling in any slide. `hinote inspect` reads that outline back.
- `hinote snapshot` checks text that overflows and the safe area. Treat what it reports as a problem of Information and text or of Layout above: first cut content, then make room. The safe area (the theme's `spacing.safe`) is the margin of one or two characters around the slide.
- Reminders and what only the speaker needs go in the slide's `notes`, not on the slide.
- `<Step>` is what Steps and animation above means. Count a slide's steps (`hinote inspect` shows them) and look for ways to use fewer.
- Align with `Row`, `Column`, and `Grid` and their numeric `gap`, and change spacing for the whole deck in the deck's CSS, which sits in its own cascade layer, rather than slide by slide. Check that edges line up by looking at the snapshot pictures.
- When the deck is done, give the speaker the checklist in `--ref delivery`.

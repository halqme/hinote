# Rehearsal, the talk, and questions

An agent cannot do any of this for the speaker, but it is worth handing over when the deck is done.

## Rehearsal

- Rehearse at least four times, ten or more as a rule, in three stages:
  1. Right after the slides are done, read the talk through once, to see that the slides do what you meant.
  2. After at least a night, a week if you can, rehearse properly, to check the story. So have the slides in shape one to two weeks before the talk.
  3. To speak smoothly, rehearse the night before and the morning of the talk.
- Where you stumble, fix the slide, not your delivery. When a part runs longer than planned, suspect too much on the slides before too little practice.
- In rehearsal, check:
  - each part takes the time planned;
  - each slide follows from the one before;
  - nothing is awkward to explain or there for nothing;
  - nothing on a slide is skipped without being explained;
  - you say nothing the slides do not show;
  - steps do not break the pace;
  - the pauses are clearly where they belong.

## Just before

- Check only the opening: the title and the first slide. Paging through the whole deck again and again is the worst thing to do: it feeds nerves and pulls you away from the audience.

## During the talk

- Mind only two things: speak loudly, and look at the audience. Aim the first words at the person farthest away. Do not worry about speaking smoothly or without mistakes.
- Do not keep watching the clock. Check it once, when you reach the last part. If you will run over, drop content rather than speed up, for example by showing the summary and ending there. Never go over the time.
- A joke is fine to catch attention in the introduction or at a pause, but not in the middle of an explanation. Falling flat is a risk, and so is landing: the talk stops, and the audience forgets where it was.

## Questions

Assume they will not go well: questions come from a vague understanding. Failing loses nothing; going well wins back points lost earlier.

- Answer a yes-or-no question with yes or no.
- Take the question literally. Do not read intent into it, and do not hear it as an attack.
- Say you do not know when you do not. Ask back when you do not understand the question.
- Do not assume the questioner understood the talk. Repeat what you said if that helps.
- If it will run long, end it with "let's talk about this afterwards".
- Exceptions: where "afterwards" is not available (a defense), where discussion is the point, or where the questioner is an expert (such as the author of the earlier work). There, put accuracy and depth before simplicity.

## Talks given online

- The same principles hold, but attention wanders more and you cannot see the audience. Prepare as if it is harder than in person.
- Record yourself once and listen: the microphone and sitting down change how your voice comes out.
- Screens show color well, but that is no reason to put more on a slide.
